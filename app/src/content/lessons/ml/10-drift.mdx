## Concept

A deployed model gives no error when the world moves. It keeps answering, with the same confidence, about complaints that may no longer look like the ones it learned from. <Term t="drift" /> is that change, and a monitor's job is to notice it without crying wolf. This lesson's honest result is that a naive monitor would have cried wolf nine times, and a careful one finds nothing it can tell apart from chance.

### Two signals

The monitor in `ml/python/monitor.py` watches the 2023–2024 stream, 50 complaints a month, in two ways:

- **Predicted-category mix:** what share of each month's complaints v1 puts in each of the six categories. It needs no labels, so a team can watch it the day a model ships.
- **Input words:** how often the month's complaints use each of the 50 most common training words, plus an "other" bucket. This catches a change in language before it shows in the predictions.

For words, 68 stop words such as "the", "my" and "was" are left out, only for drift; "not" and "no" stay, because they carry meaning in a complaint. Without the list the top 50 would be mostly function words, and their shifts say nothing about the content. With it, the top 50 hold 23% of the remaining training tokens.

### PSI

Each signal is a set of buckets: six categories, or 51 word buckets. <Term t="PSI" /> compares a period's proportions with a reference's:

PSI = Σ (a − e) × ln(a / e)

Here `e` is a bucket's share in the reference and `a` its share this period. Every term is zero or positive, so PSI is 0 when the two match and grows as they move apart. An empty bucket would make the log infinite, so `epsilon = 1e-4` is added to every proportion after dividing.

A common rule of thumb reads PSI below 0.1 as stable, 0.1 to 0.2 as worth watching, and 0.2 or more as an alert. It is a convention from credit scoring, made for coarse, decile-style bins on large samples. It is not a law, and this lesson tests it.

### The reference: the naive choice fails

The obvious reference is the data the model learned from: v1's predictions and words on its 1,800 training complaints. Against it, **9 of the 24 stream months cross 0.2**, all on the predicted-category mix, as high as 0.345 in June 2024.

Those are false alarms. v1 is 90% right on its own training complaints and 77–79% right on months it never saw. Its predictions on data it was fitted to are not its predictions on new data, so every unseen month looks drifted from in-sample behaviour. The alarm measures overfitting, not the world.

The fair reference is unseen data from just before deployment: v1's predictions and words on the 600 complaints of the 2022 test year. The word buckets are still chosen from the training years. Against 2022, **no month and no quarter crosses 0.2** on either signal. The highest monthly value is 0.192 on predictions, in June 2024.

### Is 0.2 even the right line?

With 50 complaints a month and six categories, sampling noise alone moves the predictions PSI. The monitor measures that, two ways, using only 2022, so with no drift by construction:

- **Leave-out, drawn like the sample:** 5,000 times, pick one month of 2022 at random and draw from all of 2022, without replacement, as many complaints of each category as that month has (for January 2022, 20 credit reporting, 7 debt collection, 6 each of checking, credit card and mortgage, and 5 money transfer). Compare each draw with the remaining 550. With no drift, the median predictions PSI is 0.046 and the 95th percentile 0.144; 1.4% of the draws reach 0.2.
- **Within 2022:** compare each month of 2022 with the rest of 2022. This includes the real month-to-month variation that the draws miss. The largest is 0.134 on predictions and 0.062 on words.

The calibrated threshold for each signal is the larger of the two: the leave-out 95th percentile, or the within-2022 maximum. For months that is 0.144 on predictions (the 95th percentile) and 0.062 on words (the within-2022 maximum). For quarters of 150 complaints, noise is smaller and so are the thresholds: 0.053 on predictions and 0.026 on words. Months under 30 complaints would never alert; every period here has 50 or 150.

### The noise model must match the sampling

Why copy a month's category counts instead of drawing any 50 complaints? Because that is how the sample's months were drawn. Lesson 2's quotas give every category at least 5 complaints a month and fill the rest from that month's real mix, so a real month's class mix barely varies. A random draw of 50 has no quotas: its class mix wanders, and that adds noise to the predictions PSI that real months never have.

Drawn at random, the no-drift median is 0.102 and the 95th percentile 0.306, and 15.0% of no-drift months reach 0.2. That is the right null for an unstratified production stream of 50 complaints a month: there, the 0.2 convention would fire on pure noise about one month in seven. It is the wrong null for this sample, where draws made like its months reach 0.2 only 1.4% of the time: it would set the predictions threshold twice as high as the sample's noise warrants, and miss real movement. `drift.json` keeps the random draws as `null_unstratified`, for comparison only. The words signal moves much less (95th percentile 0.047 drawn like the sample, 0.052 at random), and its threshold is the within-2022 maximum either way.

### What the calibrated monitor found

Monthly: **alerts in 3 of 24 months**. On predictions, January 2024 (0.171) and June 2024 (0.192), both above the 0.144 threshold and below 0.2; on words, November 2024 (0.082 against 0.062). Each threshold is crossed by at most 5% of no-drift months (the 95th percentile by construction; where the within-2022 maximum is higher, by fewer), so 24 months give at most 1.2 alerts per signal on average by chance. Treating months as independent, two or more on predictions happens up to 33.9% of the time with no drift, one or more on words up to 70.8%, and three or more months with an alert on either signal up to 43.6% (at most 10% a month for the two together). All of that is within what chance gives.

Quarterly: **two alerts in 8 quarters**, 2024-Q1 and 2024-Q4, both on words. At most 0.4 per signal are expected by chance, and two or more on one signal happens up to 5.7% of the time with no drift; with two signals that is up to 11.4%. That is weak evidence at most. 2024-Q1 (0.0277) barely clears a threshold (0.026) set by a single 2022 quarter, and 2024-Q4 contains the November month alert, so it is not independent evidence.

The words that moved most in 2024-Q4 were "report", "accounts" and "chase" (up) and "told" and "sent" (down). More credit-report language would fit the real growth of credit reporting below. It is a story worth checking with more data, not a finding.

The predicted-category mix alerted in two months and in no quarter: the quarters holding January and June 2024 reach 0.019 and 0.044 against a 0.053 threshold. Two monthly alerts on one signal are what chance gives up to a third of the time, so in this sample the predicted mix is still indistinguishable from noise.

### What the sample hides

The real stream did move. In the CFPB's data, credit reporting was 72.4% of these six categories' narratives in 2022 and 80.0% in 2024; mortgage fell from 3.8% to 1.5%. The course's sample is stratified, with at least 5 complaints per category every month (lesson 2), so its credit reporting share only went from 43.3% to 46.7%. Stratifying made every category learnable; it also fixed most of the class mix by design. A monitor on the real stream would see that shift in the predicted-category mix. This one cannot, and it should not pretend to.

### Label drift

Lesson 2's 2023 renames are drift of another kind: the same complaint filed under a new name. In the sample's 2023, 172 credit reporting complaints use the old name and 92 the new one. `category_map.json` maps both to today's name before training or monitoring sees them, so neither signal shows the rename. Without the map, the label set itself would have changed under the model, and no amount of watching word frequencies would have explained why.

## The code

Section 2 of `monitor.py` is PSI itself. Go recomputes it in section 6 of `ml/go/drift.go`.

<CodeTabs source="ml-monitor" section={2} goSection={6} />

- `proportions(counts, eps)` divides each count by the total, then adds `eps`. It never renormalises, so Go must do exactly the same to match.
- `contributions(expected_counts, actual_counts)` returns each bucket's `(a − e) * log(a / e)`. The monitor sorts these to find the most shifted words.
- `psi(...)` is the sum of the contributions.

Section 3 builds the buckets and the no-drift null.

<CodeTabs source="ml-monitor" section={3} pythonOnly />

- `top_words(train_rows, model_json, k=TOP_K)` picks the 50 most frequent training words that the model knows and that are not stop words; ties go alphabetically.
- `featurize(rows, words, model_json, index)` turns complaints into two count matrices: a one-hot predicted category per complaint, from `train.score`, and token counts per word bucket.
- `stratified_picks(categories, periods, size, draws, rng)` makes the draws. Each picks one 2022 period with `rng.integers`, then, for each category, ranks that category's rows by a random key and takes as many as the period has: without replacement, and with a real 2022 period's class mix. It returns one row of true/false per draw.
- `null_band(ref, dates, categories, size, unit, draws=DRAWS, seed=train.SEED)` builds the null from 5,000 of those draws, from a generator seeded with 7.
- `psi((total - c).tolist(), c.tolist())` compares each draw with the rows left over, not with all of 2022, because a stream month never overlaps the reference.
- `within = {p: _psi_vs_rest(m, periods == p) ...}` is each 2022 period against the rest of 2022.
- `"threshold": max(band["p95"], max(within.values()))` is the calibrated alert level.
- `null_band_unstratified(ref, n_rows, size)` keeps the random draws, `np.argsort(rng.random(...))[:, :size]`, for the comparison in `drift.json`. Nothing alerts on it.

Section 4 scores each period.

<CodeTabs source="ml-monitor" section={4} pythonOnly />

- `drift(stream_rows, model_json, *, reference_rows, unit, train_rows, nulls, calibrate)` groups the stream by month or quarter and scores each group against the reference.
- `words = top_words(train_rows or reference_rows, model_json)` chooses the word buckets from training, so the reference and the stream are counted on the same buckets.
- `"alert_convention": big and (p_pred >= ALERT_AT or p_words >= ALERT_AT)` is the 0.2 rule; `big` is `n >= MIN_N`, 30.
- `"alert_calibrated"` asks for PSI above that signal's null threshold instead.
- `"top_shifted_words"` keeps the five word buckets with the largest contributions and whether each went up or down.

## How Go does it

`go -C ml/go run . drift` prints the same table as `.venv-ml/bin/python ml/python/monitor.py`, character for character. It gets there partly by recomputing and partly by reading.

- **Recomputed:** Go loads the production model, picks the top words from the training split, scores and counts the 2022 reference and every stream period itself, and computes PSI and the shifted words with its own code. Its `proportions` adds epsilon after dividing, and its sums use the same `pySum` compensation as the server, so the numbers agree with Python's: to 10⁻¹² on the fixed PSI cases in the tests, and to 10⁻⁹ across the whole report.
- **Read, not recomputed:** the alert thresholds. They come from 5,000 draws of NumPy's random generator, and Go cannot reproduce NumPy's stream of random numbers. So Go reads the thresholds, settings and stop words from `drift.json` and checks it was made for the same model version and the same period sizes. It refuses to run otherwise rather than alert against the wrong line.

```python
import json
import train

d = json.loads((train.PATHS["reports"] / "drift.json").read_text())
in_sample = d["reference_training_in_sample"]["months"]
print("in-sample reference, PSI >= 0.2:", sum(m["alert_convention"] for m in in_sample), "of", len(in_sample), "months")
for unit, key in (("month", "months"), ("quarter", "quarters")):
    periods = d[key]
    conv = [p[unit] for p in periods if p["alert_convention"]]
    calib = [p[unit] for p in periods if p["alert_calibrated"]]
    print(f"2022 reference, {key}: PSI >= 0.2 in {len(conv)}, calibrated alerts in {len(calib)} {calib}")
    print(f"  expected by chance: {d['null']['expected_chance_alerts'][unit]}")
```
