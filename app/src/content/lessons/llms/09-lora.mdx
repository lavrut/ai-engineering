## Concept

Lessons 7 and 8 tuned what you send: the prompt, the model and its effort. The model itself never changed. This lesson changes the model.

### Weights, not words

A model is a very large set of numbers, its <Term t="weights" />. Every answer is computed from the input and those numbers. A prompt changes the input on each call. <Term t="fine-tuning" /> changes the weights: you show the model your examples, measure how far its answers are from the right ones, and nudge the weights to close the gap, many times over.

After fine-tuning, the behaviour lives in the model. The trained model can answer in your format with a short prompt, because it no longer needs the rules spelled out.

You cannot download Claude's weights, so this lesson uses a small open model you can train yourself: `Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct`, about half a billion weights, small enough for a free Colab GPU.

### LoRA

Training every weight means storing a gradient and optimizer state for every one of them, and saving a full copy of the model afterwards. <Term t="LoRA" /> (low-rank adaptation) avoids that. It freezes the model's weights and trains a small update beside chosen matrices.

Take one weight matrix `W`, `d × d`. LoRA adds two thin matrices: `A`, `d × r`, and `B`, `r × d`. Their product `A·B` has the same shape as `W`, and the model uses `W + A·B` in its place. Only `A` and `B` are trained. The small number `r` is the rank: `A·B` can change `W` in at most `r` independent directions.

The count is simple. `A` and `B` hold `2 · d · r` numbers; `W` holds `d²`. In this model `d = 896`. With `r = 8`, that is 14,336 trained numbers against 802,816 in `W`: 1.79%.

`lora.py` puts adapters on four attention matrices in every layer: `q_proj`, `k_proj`, `v_proj` and `o_proj`. `q_proj` and `o_proj` are 896 × 896. In this model `k_proj` and `v_proj` are 896 × 128, so their adapters are smaller. The 128 comes from the model's `config.json` on Hugging Face: 2 key-value heads of size 64. Across the whole model the trained share is well under 1%, and `print_trainable_parameters()` prints the exact count when the script runs.

The bet behind LoRA is that the change a narrow task needs is small in this sense. It often holds well enough, and it makes training fit on one small GPU.

### When fine-tuning is worth it

Fine-tuning is worth trying when a prompt cannot get you there, or costs too much to send every time:

- the behaviour is hard to put into words, but easy to show with many examples;
- the prompt is long, the volume is high, and a shorter prompt on a tuned model would cost less per call;
- you need a small model you run yourself, for cost, latency or where the data may go.

It is not worth it when prompting already clears your bar. Here it does: `v2_rules` on Haiku gets 97.5% on dev and 95.0% on test (lesson 7), for about $0.25 per 1,000 tickets (lesson 8). A prompt change takes minutes and replays for free. A fine-tune needs labelled examples, a training run and a model to host, and you redo all of it when the rules change.

### The tiny-data caveat

`lora.py` trains on the 40 dev tickets and grades on the 20 test tickets. That is far too little to call the result a tuned model:

- **About 15 optimizer steps.** 40 tickets, in batches of 4 with 2 batches added up per step, is 5 steps an epoch. Three epochs make 15 steps. An optimizer step is one update of the trained numbers.
- **20 test tickets.** The noise band is `1/√20`, ±22.4%. Any difference from Haiku's 95.0% smaller than that is not evidence.
- **No reply.** The tuned model writes only `category` and `priority`. Its `reply` is saved as an empty string, so the judge has nothing to grade, and the score covers two of the feature's three outputs.

The run shows the mechanics end to end, graded by the same code as every other configuration. It does not show what fine-tuning could reach with hundreds of real examples.

This also changes what dev is for. In lessons 7 and 8, dev was where you compared and chose. Here the dev tickets are the training data itself, so a score on dev would only show how well the model memorised them. That is why `lora.py` grades on test.

## See it

The diagram draws one 896 × 896 matrix `W` beside `A` and `B`, to scale. Watch the trained count and the bar as you move the rank from 8 down to 1, then up to 64.

<LoraDiagram initial={8} />

At `r = 8`, `A` and `B` are thin strips beside `W`: 14,336 numbers, 1.79%. At `r = 1` they are 1,792 numbers, 0.22%. At `r = 64` they are 114,688, 14.29%. The count grows in a straight line with the rank, while `W` stays frozen at 802,816. A higher rank lets the update change more, at the cost of more trained numbers.

## The code

`llms/finetune/lora.py` is Python only. It runs on a GPU in Colab, and it imports `triage_lab` to reuse the dataset, the graders and the report rather than copy them.

Section 1 is the configuration.

<CodeTabs source="lora" section={1} />

- `BASE_MODEL = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"` is the open model to fine-tune. The first run downloads about 1 GB of its weights from Hugging Face.
- `MODEL_NAME = BASE_MODEL + "+lora"` is saved on every prediction line, so a predictions file names what made it.
- `LORA_R = 8` is the rank. `LORA_ALPHA = 16` scales the update; `alpha / r = 2` is a common default.
- `TARGET_MODULES` lists the four attention matrices that get adapters. The rest of the model gets none.
- `EPOCHS = 3` passes over the 40 dev tickets. `SEED = 0` fixes the adapters' random start and the order of the data, so two runs are comparable.
- `LEARNING_RATE = 2e-4` is how big each nudge is. LoRA tolerates a higher rate than training every weight.
- `MAX_NEW_TOKENS = 32` caps each generated answer; the answer is a short JSON object.
- `SYSTEM` is the short instruction the model is trained and tested with. It names the allowed values and asks for JSON only. It has none of `v2_rules`'s rules: the examples are meant to teach them.

Section 2 turns tickets into training examples.

<CodeTabs source="lora" section={2} />

- `to_example(ticket)` pairs the ticket text with the exact completion wanted, compact JSON such as `{"category":"bug","priority":"high"}`. The labels from lesson 2 are the training targets.
- `parse_completion(text)` takes the text between the first `{` and the last `}`, parses it, and returns `None` unless both values are allowed. A small model has no structured output to enforce the shape, so the check is here.
- `chat(tokenizer, prompt, completion)` formats with the model's own chat template. Without a completion, it ends on the assistant marker so the model writes the answer.
- `prediction_line(ticket, text)` wraps the generated text as a saved-response line (lesson 4). An unparseable answer becomes an `error` line, and `reply` is always `""`.

Section 3 loads the model and adds the adapters.

<CodeTabs source="lora" section={3} />

- `AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(BASE_MODEL, torch_dtype=torch.float32)` loads the full model. At 0.5B weights, 32-bit numbers fit on a free T4 GPU.
- `LoraConfig(r=LORA_R, lora_alpha=LORA_ALPHA, target_modules=TARGET_MODULES, lora_dropout=0.05, ...)` describes the adapters. Dropout randomly switches off 5% of the adapter's inputs during training, which makes it lean less on any one of them.
- `get_peft_model(model, config)` freezes every original weight and inserts `A` and `B` beside each target matrix.
- `model.print_trainable_parameters()` prints how many numbers will train and their share of the whole.

Section 4 is the training.

<CodeTabs source="lora" section={4} />

- `tokenize(example, tokenizer)` builds the token ids for the prompt plus the completion. `labels = [-100] * len(prompt_ids) + ...` marks the prompt tokens with `-100`, which the loss ignores, so the model is trained only on writing the answer, not on repeating the ticket.
- `[:512]` cuts very long examples to 512 tokens.
- `TrainingArguments(... per_device_train_batch_size=4, gradient_accumulation_steps=2 ...)` sets 4 examples per batch and adds up 2 batches before each step. That is where 5 steps an epoch comes from.
- `logging_steps=10` prints the training loss every 10 steps, so a 15-step run prints it about once.
- `save_strategy="no"` saves no checkpoints, so the trained adapters are gone when the session ends. The predictions are what you keep.
- `DataCollatorForSeq2Seq(tokenizer, padding=True)` pads the ids and the labels in each batch to the same length.

Section 5 predicts on test and grades the answers.

<CodeTabs source="lora" section={5} />

- `model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=MAX_NEW_TOKENS, do_sample=False)` decodes greedily, always taking the likeliest next token, so the same trained model gives the same answers.
- `summarize(lines, test)` grades each line with `triage_lab.grade` (lesson 3). It prints two scores, because `accuracy` skips error lines: the share right over all 20 test tickets, with unparseable answers counted wrong, and the share right over the parsed answers only, with its noise band.
- `main` handles `--score predictions.jsonl` first, so a saved file can be graded again on any machine, with no GPU and no model download.
- `set_seed(SEED)` runs before `load_model()`, because `get_peft_model` draws the adapters' random start.
- `OUT_PATH.write_text(...)` writes `predictions.jsonl`, one saved-response line per test ticket.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Colab deletes your files">
  A Colab session's files are gone when it ends. Download `predictions.jsonl` from the file panel, or copy it to Drive, before you close the tab. It is the only result the run keeps.
</Callout>

## Try it

First, run the format tests on your own machine, from the repo root with the venv from lesson 1. They check `to_example`, `parse_completion`, `prediction_line` and the prompt masking in `tokenize`, and that `lora.py` imports without the Anthropic SDK. They need no GPU and no model:

```bash
.venv-llms/bin/python -m pytest llms/finetune -q
```

```text
9 passed
```

Then train in Google Colab. A free Colab session with a T4 GPU costs nothing, and no step calls the Claude API. The steps, as `lora.py`'s docstring gives them, take about 10 minutes:

1. In Colab, choose **Runtime > Change runtime type > T4 GPU**.
2. On your machine, zip the repo's `llms/` folder into `llms.zip`. The course repo is private, so do not paste a GitHub token into a notebook.
3. In Colab, upload `llms.zip` in the file panel, then run:

```bash
!unzip -q llms.zip
!pip install -q -r llms/finetune/requirements.txt
!python llms/finetune/lora.py
```

The first run downloads about 1 GB of model weights from Hugging Face. `predictions.jsonl` is written to the current directory: download it from the file panel, or copy it to Drive.

Here is what to look for, in order:

- the `trainable params` line from `print_trainable_parameters()`, well under 1% of all params;
- the training loss, logged once at step 10, and a summary with `train_loss` when training ends;
- `wrote predictions.jsonl (20 lines)`;
- `correct on all 20 test tickets: N/20 = P%`, counting every ticket;
- a `WARNING: ... outputs were unparseable` line, if any answer was not valid JSON with allowed values;
- `over the N parsed outputs only: category ..., priority ..., both ... (noise band +-...)`. If no output parsed at all, this line is replaced by `No output could be parsed, so there is no accuracy over parsed outputs.`, followed by a pointer to `predictions.jsonl`.

Then read the result against what you know. Haiku with `v2_rules` gets 19 of the 20 (95.0%) and `v1_basic` gets 11 (55.0%), on the same tickets. Any gap smaller than ±22.4% is not evidence either way. If many outputs were unparseable, the model has not yet learned the format, which 15 steps may not be enough for.

Back on your machine, grade the saved file again, with no GPU:

```bash
.venv-llms/bin/python llms/finetune/lora.py --score ~/Downloads/predictions.jsonl
```

Use the path where you saved the file. It prints the same lines as the end of the Colab run. To see whether the rank matters, change `LORA_R` to 4 or 16 in your copy, rerun in Colab, and compare the two `correct on all 20` lines. Expect the difference to sit inside the band: 20 tickets cannot rank two small training runs.
