## Concept

Lesson 5 ended with a number: the judge passes 14 of 20 `v2_rules` replies on `addresses_issue`. Before you act on it, you need to know whether the judge grades the way a careful person would. A code grader you can read and test. A judge you can only measure: give it replies that a person has already labelled and count how often the two agree.

### Agreement

<Term t="agreement" /> is the simplest measure: on one criterion, the share of replies where the judge's verdict equals the label. With the rubric after the reply, the judge agrees on 28 of 30 replies for `addresses_issue` (93.3%), 29 of 30 for `tone` (96.7%) and 30 of 30 for `no_false_promises` (100%).

A reply the judge could not grade, because its call failed or was refused, counts as a disagreement. None of these 30 were ungraded.

### Why agreement is not enough

Agreement ignores how often each side says pass. The drafted labels pass 24 of 30 replies on `addresses_issue`. A "judge" that says pass to everything, without reading a word, would agree 80% of the time.

Take a plainer case. If 75% of your labels are pass and the judge always says pass, it agrees on 75%. That is exactly what luck would give, so it has shown no skill at all.

<Term t="Cohen's kappa" /> corrects for that. It compares the observed agreement, `po`, with the agreement two raters would reach by chance given how often each says pass, `pe`:

```text
kappa = (po - pe) / (1 - pe)
```

Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for no better than chance and below 0 for worse than chance. The always-pass judge scores kappa 0 however high its agreement looks.

Work it through for `addresses_issue`. The judge passes 22 of 30 replies and the labels pass 24. By chance they would both say pass `(22/30) × (24/30)` of the time and both say fail `(8/30) × (6/30)` of the time, so `pe` is 0.64. `po` is 28/30, or 0.933, so kappa is `(0.933 − 0.64) / (1 − 0.64)`, or 0.81. With the rubric after the reply, kappa is 0.81 for `addresses_issue`, 0.89 for `tone` and 1.00 for `no_false_promises`. A common rough reading takes kappa above 0.8 as strong agreement.

Thirty replies is a small set. One reply is 3.3 points of agreement, and it moves kappa further: one disagreement on `no_false_promises` takes kappa from 1.00 to 0.92. Treat these as rough numbers.

### Reading the disagreements

There are three, and in each one the judge is stricter than the label:

- `r022`, `addresses_issue`: "Your account will be unlocked within the hour. Don't worry!" The customer's two-factor device is broken and they have no recovery codes. The judge fails it because the reply "ignores the broken two-factor device and missing recovery codes". The label passes it.
- `r003`, `addresses_issue`: a whole team cannot reach Lumen half an hour before a client presentation, and the reply promises a fix "within 15 minutes" and nothing else. The judge wants a way to reach the notes before the presentation.
- `r012`, `tone`: a one-line display-name question gets "We value every customer and your feedback matters greatly to us." The judge calls that dismissive boilerplate.

A disagreement is a question, not a verdict against the judge. You may well side with the judge on `r022`. That is the point of reviewing the labels yourself: sometimes the label is what was wrong.

### Position flips

Lesson 5 judged each labelled reply twice, with the rubric copy in the user message before or after the reply. A position flip is a verdict that changes between the two orders. Here there is one in 90: `r029` on `no_false_promises`, where "retry later today" is a promise in one order and a suggestion in the other.

The flip moves the numbers. With the rubric before the reply, the judge fails `r029`, disagreeing with the label, so `no_false_promises` agreement is 29 of 30 and kappa 0.92. With it after, it is 30 of 30 and 1.00. The other two criteria are identical in both orders.

A flip does not say which order is right. It marks a verdict that position can tip, so a close call. A few flips are normal. Many would mean the layout of the prompt, not the reply, is deciding verdicts. You would then fix one order for every call, or run both and treat a disagreement between them as "unsure".

### When agreement is low

Work through it in this order:

1. **Read the disagreements and the judge's reasons.** Decide for each one whether the label or the judge is wrong.
2. **Fix wrong labels without looking at the judge.** Re-read the reply against the rubric, not the verdict. A label changed to match the judge makes agreement measure nothing, the same mistake as the `t047` rule in lesson 2. The labelling page below does not show the judge's verdicts, for this reason.
3. **Sharpen a vague criterion.** If the judge and you read a criterion differently, write down what it means. For example: does "retry later today" count as a promise? Split a criterion that mixes two questions.
4. **Change the judge.** Add a passing and a failing example to the judge prompt, or try a stronger model.
5. **Measure again on labels you did not tune on.** Rewriting the judge until it matches these 30 makes agreement on these 30 optimistic, like tuning on the test set. Label a fresh set for the final check.
6. **Stop trusting that criterion if it stays low.** Have people grade it, and keep the judge for the criteria where it agrees.

## The code

Section 7 is the three measures.

<CodeTabs source="llms" section={7} goNote="The same three functions. A verdict is a *bool, nil when the judge gave none, so the comparisons look different; the section below explains why." />

- `agreement(judge_v, human_v, criterion)` counts the positions where the two lists hold the same value and divides by the length. A `None` from the judge never equals `True` or `False`, so an ungraded reply counts as a disagreement.
- `if len(judge_v) != len(human_v) or not judge_v` raises a `ValueError` naming the criterion. Two lists of different lengths mean the replies are out of step, and any number computed from them would be wrong.
- `cohen_kappa(a, b)` computes `po`, the observed agreement, and `pa` and `pb`, how often each rater says `True`.
- `pe = pa * pb + (1 - pa) * (1 - pb)` is the chance that two raters with those rates agree by luck: both say pass, or both say fail.
- `if pe == 1` handles raters who each give one answer every time. Kappa is undefined there, since `1 - pe` is 0, so the code returns 1.0 if they also agree and 0.0 if not.
- `position_flips(before_v, after_v)` counts the replies whose verdict differs between the two orders.
- `cmd_agreement` in section 10 calls these per criterion and per order. It passes `[bool(x) for x in judge_v]` to kappa, which needs booleans, so an ungraded verdict counts as a fail there.

## How Go does it

**Three states in a pointer.** A verdict can be pass, fail or missing. Python uses `True`, `False` or `None`. Go's `bool` has no third value, so `verdicts` returns `map[string]*bool`: a pointer to a bool, `nil` when the judge gave no verdict.

**Comparing values, not pointers.** For two pointers, `x == y` is true only when they point at the same variable, whatever the bools hold. Two separate `true` values would count as different. So the comparisons dereference with `*`, and check for `nil` first, because dereferencing a `nil` pointer panics:

- `agreement` counts a match only when `j != nil && *j == humanV[i]`, so a `nil` is a disagreement, as in Python.
- `positionFlips` counts a flip when `(x == nil) != (y == nil) || (x != nil && *x != *y)`: one side is missing and the other is not, or both are present and differ.
- `asBools` turns `[]*bool` into `[]bool` with `nil` as `false`, the same as Python's `bool(None)`, before calling `cohenKappa`.

`go run . agreement` from `llms/go` prints the same table as Python.

Now see what kappa does to a judge that always says pass. Save this as `llms/python/kappa_demo.py`:

```python
import triage_lab as L

labels = [r["labels"] for r in L.load_jsonl(L.DATA_DIR / "reply_labels.jsonl")]
for rid in L.RUBRIC_IDS:
    human = [l[rid] for l in labels]
    always_pass = [True] * len(human)
    print(f"{rid:<18} labels pass {sum(human)}/{len(human)}  "
          f"agreement {L.agreement(always_pass, human, rid):.1%}  kappa {L.cohen_kappa(always_pass, human):.2f}")
```
